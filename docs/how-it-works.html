How it works

A denial letter arrives. Nine steps later, a person signs.

Overturn does the part a billing clerk cannot afford to do well: read the payer’s own policy, check the chart against it criterion by criterion, and write the appeal in the payer’s language. It stops before the part only a person should do.

The problem

A clinic gets a denial letter. Somewhere in it is a policy number. Appealing it properly is forty minutes of skilled work for one claim — and the claim is often worth less than the forty minutes. So most denials are never appealed. Not because the care was wrong, but because nobody had forty minutes.

The hard part is not the writing. A language model will happily write a persuasive appeal citing a policy section that does not exist, or quoting a chart note the patient never had. A fabricated citation is worse than no appeal at all: it costs the clinic credibility with the payer on every future claim. Overturn is built around that failure, not around the drafting.

The pipeline

Seven agents, each with a job narrow enough to be checked. An orchestrator moves the case between them. Every step writes to the same case record, so the whole path is auditable afterward.

  1. Sentinel screens the document

    Before anything reads the letter as instructions, three layers scan it as data: deterministic rules, Google Cloud Model Armor, and Gemma running as a guard model. A letter carrying an injected instruction is quarantined here and goes no further. CASE-002 is the one that gets caught.

  2. Intake extracts the claim

    Patient, procedure code, denial reason, and the policy identifier the payer cited — pulled out as structured fields. Intake is instructed to leave a field null rather than guess: a null is recoverable, an invented claim number is not.

  3. Retrieval finds the policy

    Looks up the payer’s published medical policy by the identifier in the letter. If no policy covers the denial, the case stops here and is marked declined_no_basis — there is nothing honest to argue.

  4. Mapping checks the chart, criterion by criterion

    Each criterion in the policy gets a verdict against the record: satisfied, not satisfied, or not documented — with the chart locator that supports it. Negation is handled explicitly, including when it distributes over a list (“denies homicidal ideation, intent or plan” is three negatives, not one).

  5. Drafting writes the appeal

    The only agent that writes prose, and the only one on the heavier model tier. It sees the criteria matrix and the retrieved policy text — nothing else.

    Drafting cannot retrieve. It has no tool that reaches the policy store or the chart. If a fact is not in the matrix it was handed, it cannot go and get it, so an invented fact has no source to hide behind.

  6. Verification tries to break the draft

    A separate agent on a separate model checks every claim in the letter against the source. Each policy citation must resolve to a real section id. Each chart quote must resolve to a real locator. Anything unsupported is a rejection with a written reason, and the draft goes back to step 5 with those reasons attached.

    Verification cannot write. It can reject and explain. It cannot edit the letter into passing.

  7. The retry loop, with a hard cap

    Three attempts. If the draft still does not pass, Verification’s reasons are carried onto the case, the case moves to needs_human_review, and no appeal is sent. Failing to send is the designed outcome, not an error. This fired on a real model.

  8. Two people sign

    A clerk confirms the paper trail. A clinician co-signs the medicine. Both signatures must land on the same draft attempt — revise the letter and both signatures are void. Until then the case cannot be submitted. In the deployment, CASE-005 sat with one signature on it and went nowhere until the second arrived.

  9. Then it waits — for weeks

    Lifecycle holds the case after submission, tracks the payer’s response clock, and escalates to the next appeal level on its own when the deadline passes without an answer. This is the part a clerk actually loses: the follow-up six weeks later that nobody diarised.

    This has run unattended in the deployment. CASE-006 was submitted, the payer stayed silent, the window lapsed, and Lifecycle moved it from first_level_appeal to peer_to_peer_review with nobody asking. The deployment runs a compressed clock — one second standing in for one day — so an escalation is watchable rather than a month away. See it in the queue.
